What the altitude actually costs you

  • Pressure ratio rises. Boost is measured as gauge pressure, so to hold 25 psi gauge at 2,100 ft the compressor has to work to a higher ratio (2.92 vs 2.73). That alone adds about 12 °C to compressor outlet temperature.
  • Mass flow drops. Same manifold absolute pressure means less absolute air — roughly 7% less than the sea-level equivalent, so slightly less power but also slightly less heat to reject.
  • Cooling air is thinner. The ambient side of the core carries ~7% less mass at the same face velocity, which nudges effectiveness down by ~1–2 points. Small.
  • Net: the altitude penalty is real but modest, and it pushes in the direction of more core, not less — which the recommendation already reflects.
Western NC is also a mountain-road environment: long sustained climbs at 40–60 mph in 3rd/4th gear. That is a genuinely demanding intercooler duty cycle — high load, moderate airspeed, no cool-down — and it is much closer to the "sustained load" regime where tube-and-fin's advantage appears than a typical flat-land street car. It is the strongest single argument against my bar-and-plate recommendation. See §06 and §15.

04The math, shown

Six equations get you from "2.2 litres at 25 psi" to "this core, this size." Every number in this report comes out of these, and they are the same equations the calculators in §05 run live.

Step 1 — Air mass flow

A four-stroke fills its displacement once every two revolutions. Volumetric efficiency (VE) scales that; the ideal gas law converts volume to mass.

V̇ = (D / 1000) × (RPM / 2) / 60 × VE m³/s at manifold conditions ρman = P_MAP × 1000 / (R × (T_IAT + 273.15)) R = 287.05 J/kg·K ṁ = V̇ × ρman kg/s Your numbers: D = 2.2 L, RPM = 7000, VE = 0.95, MAP = 266 kPa abs, IAT = 62 °C → ṁ = 0.345 kg/s = 45.7 lb/min = 357 CFM at compressor outlet conditions

Step 2 — Compressor outlet temperature

Isentropic compression heats the air; real compressor inefficiency heats it more. ηc is compressor isentropic efficiency — read off the compressor map at your PR and flow.

PR = P_MAP / (P_amb × 0.97) 0.97 = 3% intake/filter loss T₂ = T₁ × [ 1 + ( PR^((γ−1)/γ) − 1 ) / ηc ] γ = 1.40, T in kelvin Your numbers: P_amb = 93.9 kPa (2,100 ft), P_MAP = 266.2 kPa, PR = 2.92, ηc = 0.74 → T₂ = 180 °C (356 °F). That is the air arriving at the intercooler.

Step 3 — Effectiveness and heat load

Effectiveness (ε) is the only intercooler metric worth quoting, because it is independent of ambient temperature. "80% efficient" from a vendor means ε = 0.80.

ε = (T_in − T_out) / (T_in − T_amb) T_out = T_in − ε × (T_in − T_amb) Q = ṁ × cp × (T_in − T_out) cp = 1005 J/kg·K At ε = 0.80: T_out = 180 − 0.80 × (180 − 32) = 61.6 °C (143 °F) Q = 0.345 x 1005 x 125 = 43.5 kW = 148,000 BTU/hr That is roughly the heat output of two household furnaces, dumped into a 610 mm box.

Step 4 — ε-NTU, the first-principles check

Rules of thumb give you a size. ε-NTU tells you whether that size actually achieves the effectiveness you assumed. NTU is the exchanger's thermal size; Cr is the ratio of the two streams' heat capacities.

C_hot = ṁ_charge × cp C_cold = ṁ_ambient × cp Cmin = min(C_hot, C_cold) Cr = Cmin / Cmax A = V_core × β β ≈ 900 m²/m³ for a brazed CAC NTU = U × A / Cmin U ≈ 55 × (v_face/10)^0.5 W/m²K, air-side limited Cross-flow, both fluids unmixed (the correct case for a bar-and-plate CAC): ε = 1 − exp[ (NTU^0.22 / Cr) × ( exp(−Cr × NTU^0.78) − 1 ) ]
The two methods agree. The CSF 8067 (610 × 300 × 75 mm, 13.7 L core volume) returns ε = 0.848 at 12 m/s face velocity and ε = 0.93 at 22 m/s. The vendor rates it 850 hp against your 450 hp demand. Independent methods, same conclusion: this core is comfortably sufficient, with margin for heat soak.

Step 5 — Charge pipe velocity

A = π × (d/2)² d in metres ρ = P × 1000 / (R × (T + 273.15)) use LOCAL P and T, not ambient v = ṁ / (ρ × A) Critical detail most calculators get wrong: hot-side and cold-side density differ by ~40%. Same pipe, same mass flow, very different velocity. Hot side is always faster.

Step 6 — Pressure drop

ΔP = K × ½ ρ v² K = f × L/d + ΣK_bend f ≈ 0.02 (smooth aluminium), K_bend ≈ 0.25 per mandrel 90° at R/D ≥ 1.5 Core ΔP scales roughly with flow squared — scale from a vendor's published rating point: ΔP = ΔP_ref × (CFM / CFM_ref)²
Why ΔP matters less than people think, and more than they admit. Pressure lost across the cooler must be made up by the compressor working to a higher ratio, which heats the air further — a self-defeating loop. But the loop is weak: my model says 3 psi of total ΔP raises compressor outlet by only 12 °C and post-cooler IAT by 2.4 °C. The real cost of ΔP is not heat, it is turbo shaft speed and spool — you burn compressor map to push against your own plumbing. Budget for it, but do not sacrifice core volume chasing it.

Why the disagreement is physically coherent

All three results can be true at once, because the two constructions differ in two independent ways that dominate at different timescales.

Thermal mass — wins the first 30 seconds

Bar-and-plate uses solid extruded bars separating stacked plates. More aluminium per litre — my model puts a 610×300×75 mm bar-plate core at ~28 kg vs ~19 kg for the same volume in tube-and-fin. That is 25.3 kJ/K of thermal capacity vs 16.7 kJ/K.

Practically: one 8-second WOT pull raises the bar-plate core metal about 6.9 °C; the same pull raises the tube-fin core 10.6 °C. On pull one and two, bar-and-plate is a thermal battery and the charge air rides on cold metal. This is exactly what PRL measured.

Surface-to-mass ratio — wins the long game

A tube wall is thinner than a bar. Once the metal is saturated, what matters is how fast heat gets out of the metal into the ambient air. Thinner walls and higher surface-per-kilogram mean a shorter time constant — my estimate is ~5.5 s for tube-and-fin vs ~8.4 s for bar-and-plate at the same core volume.

Practically: by pull five or six the battery is flat and the tube-fin core has become the better radiator. This is why PRL's curves cross.

The decision for your car

Arguments for bar & plate (my recommendation)

  • Street driving is transient: 5–15 second bursts with minutes of light load between. That is entirely inside the thermal-battery regime where bar-plate leads.
  • "Occasional track" plus "very little drag racing" means you rarely reach pull 6.
  • Far wider availability of bare cores in the exact size you need. Tube-and-fin bare cores in 610×300×75 essentially do not exist from reputable makers — CSF, Garrett, Treadstone and Vibrant all sell bar-and-plate cores; CSF's tube-fin technology only ships in vehicle-specific assemblies.
  • Welding tapered tanks onto a bar-and-plate end sheet is straightforward. Tube-and-fin header plates are thinner and less forgiving of a fabricator's heat.
  • Higher burst pressure margin at 25+ psi. CSF pressure-test their cores at 120 psi.

Arguments against — taken seriously

  • Western NC mountain roads. A three-mile 6% climb at 50 mph in third is a sustained-load event, not a transient one. That is genuinely tube-and-fin's home turf and it is the best reason to disagree with me.
  • Weight. ~9 kg saved, all of it ahead of the front axle line on a nose-heavy AWD car. Not nothing for a car you also want to handle.
  • ΔP. Tube-and-fin generally runs lower internal restriction for the same face size — larger internal volume, lower air velocity through the core.
  • Recovery between track sessions. Faster time constant means it is cool again sooner in the paddock.

If any of those become your priority, switch. The recommendation is not a religious position — see §15 for exactly what would change my answer.

Perspective on magnitude. Every effect in this section is worth roughly 5–15 °C. Going from unducted to properly sealed ducting (§09) is worth 25–35 °C on the same core. If you have finite effort, spend it on the duct.

08End tank design — the part that actually decides performance

You require single-pass, side inlet and side outlet, for packaging. Good news: by Garrett's own analysis that is the best configuration available, not a compromise.

Garrett, on manifold shape: "The side entry is ideal for both pressure drop and flow distribution, but it is usually not possible due to vehicle space limitations." Your packaging constraint has handed you the optimum. Do not fight it.

The problem end tanks solve

A core is a bank of parallel tubes. Air takes the path of least resistance, which means a badly-shaped tank sends most of the flow through a handful of tubes while the rest sit nearly stagnant. Two things go wrong: the effective core is much smaller than the one you paid for, and the busy tubes run at high velocity, so ΔP climbs. This is called maldistribution, and it is the single most common reason a big intercooler underperforms a small one.

Over-the-top entry

Air enters the top and must turn 90° downward. Garrett: "can starve the top tubes." Momentum carries flow to the far end. Worst

Centre entry, box tank

Jet impinges on the header plate and recirculates. Centre tubes overworked, ends dead. Cheap eBay standard. Poor

Side entry, tapered Yours

Flow enters parallel to the tube bank. A taper that narrows along the flow direction keeps static pressure roughly constant along the header, so every tube sees the same driving pressure. Best

09Fin density and ducting

Fin density (FPI)

FPI is a straight trade: more fins = more surface area = more heat transfer, but also more blockage = more air-side pressure drop, which slows the air through the core and starves everything behind it.

FPI trade-off — heat transfer, ΔP, and the ratio

Heat transfer Q (relative) Air-side ΔP (relative) Q per unit ΔP (efficiency)

Normalised to 16 FPI = 1.0. Q scales roughly as FPI^0.55 (diminishing — extra fins get less effective as fin efficiency drops), ΔP roughly linearly. Published research on finned air coolers finds the Q/Δp ratio peaks around 12 FPI, and this model agrees: Q/ΔP falls monotonically above ~12 FPI.

The Q/ΔP peak is not the right answer for a car. That metric optimises a fan-driven cooler where you pay for the pressure drop directly. On a moving car the "fan" is free ram air, so you can afford some ΔP to get more Q — up to the point where the pressure drop starves whatever sits behind the core. On your car, that is the radiator and the A/C condenser. That constraint, not the Q/ΔP peak, sets your FPI.

The radiator shadowing problem

A front-mount intercooler sits in front of the radiator and does two bad things to it: it blocks some air, and it pre-heats the rest. Your ST185 already has a 690 × 325 mm radiator core plus an A/C condenser plus a Chase Bays 10-row oil cooler wanting the same air.

Air temperature entering the radiator

At the design point the intercooler dumps 40 kW into roughly 2.4 kg/s of ambient air passing through it. That air leaves the intercooler about 16 °C hotter than it arrived (40,000 / (2.4 × 1005) ≈ 16.6 K).

So the radiator, which used to see 32 °C air, now sees ~48 °C air behind the intercooler's shadow. Its available ΔT for cooling 90 °C coolant drops from 58 K to 42 K — a 28% reduction in cooling driving force in the shadowed region.

What to do about it

  • Choose 14–16 FPI, not 20+. A dense core is a wall. 14–16 FPI keeps air-side ΔP moderate so the radiator still gets flow.
  • Do not full-shadow the radiator. A 610 mm intercooler in front of a 690 mm radiator core leaves ~40 mm of unshadowed core on each side, if you centre it. Keep that.
  • Height matters more than width. A 300 mm intercooler in front of a 325 mm radiator shadows nearly all of it vertically. Mounting the intercooler as low as the bumper allows preserves a strip of clean air to the radiator's top tank region.
  • Air must have somewhere to go. Seal the top of the rad support and vent the engine bay. A bumper that ingests air with no exit path produces a high-pressure zone that stalls flow through both coolers.
  • Expect higher coolant temps and plan for them. This is a real cost of any FMIC and the honest answer is you accept it, not that you engineer it away.

Ducting — the highest-value thing on this list

You said you plan custom ducting. Good. On an unducted core, most of the air that approaches the front simply goes around it — the core is a resistance, and air is lazy. Ducting turns "air near the core" into "air through the core."

Quantify the prize. Should you mix diameters?

The folklore

"Run a smaller hot side and a bigger cold side. Hot air is less dense so it needs more room / cold air is denser so it flows better."

Some versions claim the larger cold side "allows the gas to expand without a bottleneck"; others claim smaller hot side "keeps velocity up for spool."

What the physics says

The mass flow is identical on both sides. The hot side has lower density, so for equal diameters it already runs ~30% faster and therefore has higher ΔP per metre. If you were going to make one side smaller for velocity reasons, it would be the cold side, not the hot — the exact opposite of the folklore.

In practice the difference is small enough that a single diameter is right, and it makes couplers, clamps and spares interchangeable.

The one legitimate reason to change diameter: the throttle body. Your Bosch 74 mm motorsport throttle body has a bore of 2.91 in. Garrett's guidance — never step down into a component, and avoid sharp area changes — means the final section of cold pipe should taper from 2.5 in up to ~2.9 in over the last 100–150 mm before the throttle. Keep that taper's half-angle under 7°, same diffuser rule as everything else. Do not run 2.9 in all the way from the intercooler: that adds ~0.9 L of dead volume for no flow benefit.

Volume and throttle response — how much does it really cost?

Every litre of charge-side volume must be filled to boost pressure before the manifold sees it. Bigger pipe = more volume = slightly slower boost onset and recovery between shifts.

System volume, 2.5 in build: hot pipe 1.1 m × 1.58 L/m = 1.74 L cold pipe 1.5 m × 1.58 L/m = 2.37 L core internal (13.7 L core, ~28% void) = 3.84 L end tanks 2 × 0.55 L = 1.10 L --------------------------------------------- TOTAL = 9.05 L ~4.1× engine displacement Going 2.5 → 3.0 in on both pipes adds 1.82 L (+20% system volume). Going 2.5 → 2.25 in saves 0.78 L (−9%) but costs ~0.35 psi of ΔP.

A useful sanity check: total charge volume of 3–5× engine displacement is normal and healthy for a front-mount. You are at 4.1×. Above roughly 6× the lag becomes noticeable on a small-displacement engine. You have room, so optimise for ΔP and let the volume be what it is.

Bends and routing

  • Mandrel bends only. Crush bends neck the tube down and create a permanent restriction.
  • Bend radius R/D ≥ 1.5 — Garrett's published standard. For 2.5 in pipe that is a 95 mm centreline radius minimum.
  • Budget ~1% pressure loss per 90° bend. A typical FMIC route has 6–8 of them. Every bend you delete is free power.
  • No sharp transitions. Where you must change section, use a taper — and again, ≤7°.
  • Keep the hot pipe away from the manifold and downpipe, or wrap/coat it. On a transverse 3S-GTE the hot pipe runs right past the turbine housing. This is one place a thermal barrier coating genuinely earns its keep.
  • BOV placement: your Turbosmart Kompact Dual Port goes on the cold side, as close to the throttle body as practical, so it vents the whole charge volume on lift.

17.1  Why the earlier rpm answer was wrong

Engine airflow is displacement × rpm × volumetric efficiency × charge density. Volumetric efficiency (VE) is the only term in that product that is not a hard number, so the whole rpm question comes down to which VE curve you believe.

Round one used a curve that peaks at 5,500 rpm and falls away fast: VE 0.93 at 6,650 rpm, 0.84 at 7,200, 0.70 at 7,800. Nothing was cited for it. Under that curve, revving from 6,650 to 7,200 loses power, because VE falls faster than rpm rises. That is where "raising rpm will not help" came from.

Round two derives the curve instead. The standard first-principles test for whether a cylinder head runs out of breath is the Taylor inlet Mach index, which needs only bore, stroke, intake valve size and rpm — all of which are known here:

bore 87.5 mm → piston area 6,013 mm² 2 × 33.5 mm intake valves → nominal valve area 1,763 mm² (3S-GTE, Ferrea standard size) area ratio Ap/Ai 3.41 Z = Ap · Ūp / (Ci · Ai · a), Ci = 0.40, a = 360 m/s at 50 °C 6,650 rpm → Z = 0.48 flat — head is not the limit 7,200 rpm → Z = 0.52 just past the knee 7,800 rpm → Z = 0.56 falling 8,400 rpm → Z = 0.60 clearly restricted

Z stays under 0.5 through 7,000 rpm. The 3S-GTE head is not the thing stopping this engine at 6,650 rpm. Round two's VE curve is that Mach rolloff applied to a broad cam curve, with a residual-gas penalty for exhaust backpressure. The headline numbers use an exhaust-to-intake pressure ratio of 1.6, which is typical for a well-matched turbine. The chart shows the whole band.

18.3  Go / no-go

NO-GO on machining billet end tanks. Five reasons, in order of weight:
  • It buys nothing over cast. The only real advantage billet has over fabricated sheet is that you can machine internal diffuser vanes. Cast tanks already have them, and a fully assembled off-the-shelf intercooler comes with either cast tanks or professionally developed fabricated ones.
  • It is heavier and it soaks heat. 2.13 kg against 0.92 kg for fabricated, and 1,913 J/K against 832 J/K. That aluminium sits directly in the hot charge stream. Every joule of it has to be reheated on each pull and dumped between pulls. Billet more than doubles the tank heat capacity for no cooling benefit.
  • The joint to the core is the failure mode, and billet does not help it. Every one of these methods ends the same way: an aluminium tank TIG-welded to the core header. Boost cycling fatigues that weld. A billet tank is welded exactly like a fabricated one. You have moved the cost without moving the risk.
  • 12 machine-hours plus $334 of stock, for a one-off. Even valuing your machine time at zero, that is 12 hours you could spend on the ducting, which the round-one model says is worth about 27 °C — three times the difference between any two core choices in this report.
  • You said you prefer off-the-shelf. Nothing in the engineering argues against you.
Where to spend the shop machine time instead, in priority order:
  • The duct. A properly sealed shroud from the bumper aperture to the core face, four edges sealed, 7° per wall. Worth about 27 °C of intake air temperature on the model in §19. Nothing else on the car comes close to that return per hour.
  • The charge-pipe-to-throttle-body adapter, with the M12 IAT boss placed in the airstream. One setup, an hour, and it is the part that lets you measure whether any of this report is true.
  • Mounting brackets and the crash-bar interface. A 610 mm core in an ST185 bumper needs real brackets, and the 1×1 in aluminium bar gives you something to work with.

21.0  The single error that explains most of the gap

Before the item-by-item list, one finding underlies items 1, 2, 3 and 9. 06_turbo_model.py computes airflow as

lb/min = K × RPM × VE × PR K = 0.0029233, PR = (boost + 14.7) / 14.7

Rebuilding K from its parts shows what it contains: K = (2164 cc ÷ 2) × 0.0765 lb/ft³. That density, 0.0765 lb/ft³, is dry air at 15 °C and sea level. Multiplying it by pressure ratio and nothing else means the model is assuming the air arriving at the intake valve is at 15 °C, at every boost level, forever.

The prior model has no charge temperature in it and no altitude in it. Working the implied manifold temperature back out of K × PR returns exactly 15 °C at 20, 25 and 30 psi alike. The real charge temperature at this car's design point is about 79 °C after a 610 × 305 × 76 mm core at 32 °C ambient. Air at 79 °C is 18% less dense than air at 15 °C. The prior model also uses 14.7 psi as ambient, which is sea level; Weaverville is 93.87 kPa (13.61 psi). Together those two omissions inflate every flow figure in the prior research by roughly 15%, and every power figure by the same 15% again on top of the conversion-constant error in item 3.

21.3  Item 3 — the conversion constants

21.11  Item 11 — boost control plumbing

The prior research is right, the reasoning is sound, and this is now a build note.

The rule: feed the MAC 46A boost-control solenoid from a pre-throttle charge-pressure source — a 1/8 NPT bung in the charge pipe between the intercooler and the throttle body. Do not feed it from the billet distribution block on the intake manifold.

Why, checked step by step

#StepSound?
1The Turbosmart GenV IWG75 Twin Port actuator has two chambers. The top port assists opening; the bottom port assists holding closed against boost. A twin-port actuator needs positive pressure on the bottom port to hold the gate shut above spring pressure. Correct — this is what makes twin-port different from single-port, and your invoice confirms a TS-0620-4012 Twin Port with a 14 psi spring.
2The MAC 46A four-port solenoid distributes its supply pressure between those two chambers. It cannot create pressure; it can only route what it is fed. Correct
3On a lift or a shift the throttle closes. Manifold pressure goes to vacuum within milliseconds. The charge pipe upstream of the throttle stays pressurised. Correct — that pressure difference is the entire reason a blow-off valve exists.
4If the solenoid is fed from the manifold, both actuator chambers lose supply at that moment. The actuator spring shuts the wastegate fully while the turbo is still spinning near its previous speed.Correct
5On re-application the gate is already shut and the solenoid has no pressure yet to modulate with, so boost overshoots before control authority returns. Correct. This is a well-documented failure mode. It is worst on twin-port internal gates, exactly what you have.

22The twin-scroll manifold is paired wrong

The prior research says your twin-scroll manifold pairs cylinders 1+2 / 3+4, and that the correct pairing is 1+4 / 2+3. This was checked independently, from firing order and cam duration alone, without reference to the prior work. It is correct.

Verified. On a 1-3-4-2 firing order with the HKS 264° exhaust cam, pairing 1+2 and 3+4 puts 84° of crank angle where both cylinders in a scroll have their exhaust valves open at once. Pairing 1+4 and 2+3 gives a 96° clear gap in each scroll with no overlap at all. The prior research's figures reproduce exactly.

22.1  The arithmetic, from first principles

Nothing here needs a simulation. Firing order and exhaust duration are enough.

firing order 1-3-4-2, so power strokes begin at: cyl 1 = 0° cyl 3 = 180° cyl 4 = 360° cyl 2 = 540° HKS 264 exhaust cam: 264° duration, exhaust valve opens about 135° after firing TDC cyl 1 exhaust open 135° → 399° cyl 3 exhaust open 315° → 579° cyl 4 exhaust open 495° → 759° (= 495° → 720° and 0° → 39°) cyl 2 exhaust open 675° → 939° (= 675° → 720° and 0° → 219°) Two cylinders 180° apart in the cycle: 264 − 180 = 84° of OVERLAP Two cylinders 360° apart in the cycle: 360 − 264 = 96° of CLEAR GAP YOUR PAIRING 1+2 are 180° apart → 84° overlap 3+4 are 180° apart → 84° overlap CORRECT 1+4 are 360° apart → 96° gap 2+3 are 360° apart → 96° gap

The general rule, which matches published twin-scroll guidance: pair the cylinder that fires first with the one that fires third, and the second with the fourth. On 1-3-4-2 that is 1 with 4, and 3 with 2. Your manifold pairs adjacent-in-time cylinders instead, which is the one arrangement that guarantees collision.

22.2  What the wrong pairing actually costs

The purpose of a divided turbine housing is to keep the blowdown pulse from one cylinder away from another cylinder that is still trying to empty. When both cylinders in a scroll are open together, the opening cylinder's blowdown pressurises the scroll while its neighbour is mid-stroke, and that pressure pushes back into the neighbour's cylinder.
